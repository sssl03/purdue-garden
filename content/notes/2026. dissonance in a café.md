two days ago, i went for dinner to [[sproute cafe]] with [[miss]]. i've spent both days since then writing this.

—

sproute cafe has decent ambience. the food is good : the desserts better. for some reason though, i feel uneasy there.

when i read about the cafe being supported by a consulting company (in their menu), i wonder if my unease is caused by that. what if the quality of food is only temporarily inflated by support from the consulting company or venture capital? what if i'm only being duped into thinking it's a good cafe? once they have large-enough a "captive audience" and decide to capitalise on their profits, am i going to be disappointed by them then? that's what happened with so many other cafes and chains in the past, after all.

i realise that this sounds incredibly prejudiced. running cafes or restaurants is tough, and it is likely a good thing if a cafe seeks support from consultants or capitalists who can help it not only survive but also succeed and even thrive. even so: the unease i feel at this cafe feels similar to the unease several other things make me feel now, and so i wish to explore this feeling.

i've felt similarly while watching youtube-channels like veritasium and animalogic too. animalogic, especially, used to endear to me (a lot), but at some point i began to feel distant from it. everything about the videos still seemed just-about-right, but they didn't invoke the same _affect_ they used to before. instead: i tired of their videos easily, trusted their content (or recommendations) less, and felt less frictious[^1] when electing to watch something else instead. their videos felt a tad-bit less authentic[^5]. even though i continued to watch their videos, my attachment to them had frayed. when i finally learned about them having been acquired by venture capital (and realised that the beginning of my unease had coincided with them getting gobbled-up[^2]), the frayed threads swiftly severed, and i stopped watching their newer videos altogether.

i hope this cafe is not influenced by its consulting company in any similar way. 

truthfully, everything about the cafe seems just fine. there's quite some art on shelves and walls; and though the pieces seem arbitrary, none of it is jarring or ugly. the lighting is warm; the furniture could have felt softer, but it's quite fine as is. sometimes we need to get up to get the staff's attention, but pressing buttons on table-top gizmos almost always does the trick. as you can see: nothing to complain about; quite above average even! but yet, nothing really invokes an _affect_ in me. even though the pieces seem well-placed, the whole doesn't feel greater than the sum its parts. when i really think about it, i can't pin-point anything that could make the cafe endear to me. everything feels just a tiny bit less-than-authentic; as if the cafe's trying a bit too hard to look like an intimate space, but isn't 100% succeeding at *being* one. 

(on the other hand, [[udupi shree darshini]] hasn't made me feel uneasy in the same way even though its staff can be inattentive, its wall are bland, and its lighting is functional at best.)

the cafe is transparent about its affiliation with a restaurant consulting firm ; and i'm sure i'm wrongly latching on to that to explain my unease. because: if i get upset at a cafe for declaring its benefactors, i might actually disincentivise other cafes from being honest about their affiliations. i might also just be prejudiced against venture capital and the notion of consulting (due to my experiences with these in the past), and it is likely that i am unfairly mixing up my feelings about that with this small cafe. 

it might also be that i expect an intimate space to behave in a certain way (eg. when we go to pay the bill, perhaps the owner smiles and makes eye-contact, and asks how the food was — and does so out of genuine care), and am just throwing a tantrum when things don't happen that way. i don't know.

but i must listen to my gut: i'm feeling _something_ about this experience that i need to pay attention to, even if i lack the ability to understand it or articulate through words right away. 

so, what could it be? maybe the cafe is trying too hard to be something it inherently isn't? 

(for some reason, the word 'growth' buzzes into my mind as i write this, so i will dwell on that next. i hope it'll all tie together in the end.)

it seems to me that growth, of any kind, comes at a cost.[^7] what is that cost? ; what must we lose when we grow? a common occurrence, in my experience, is an erosion in our ability to care about the things that made us start the project in the first place. for a cafe, it may result in a talkative founder not being able to indulge in pleasantry with patrons because their focus shifts to bigger revenues and long-term growth. 

i realise that growth can be viewed though a lens of "shifting priorities" instead of the lens of loss or compromise. after all, everyone has a finite ability to pay attention to things. generally speaking, being more careful about one part of a business will inevitably take it away from another. so, from the cafe's point of view, their choices may seem fair. 

the customer, however, receives a diminished experience irrespective of the cafe's reasons for it. the cafe's choices result in subtle (but real) differences between the idea that patrons have of a cafe and what their actual experience ends up being[^5]. 

(i guess most people don't overthink "going to a cafe" in this manner, so it may not matter to them. i, on the other hand, seem to romanticise the idea of a thing, and then am over-sensitive to the dissonance between that idea and the reality i experience. i may eventually need to "accept reality" or "deal with it and move on", i guess, but that day is not today.)

you see,

when we go for a magic show, even though we know magic is not real, we're happy to suspend our disbelief because we want to have a good time. the magician must work very hard to hide all the lies, though, because a single tear in the experience shatters the illusion. this concept of 'suspension of disbelief' may apply to any experience, i think, whether eating at a cafe, working in a company, or watching a youtube video.

this cafe was a convenient go-to place for us, and generally-speaking quite nice. today, unfortunately, the food and the dessert were not as delicious as we expected them to be. for some reason, even just that much seemed to shatter my _suspension of disbelief_: the food suddenly felt too expensive, the management too aloof, the art meaningless, the air-conditioning too strong, the food too spicy, and the ambience too yellow too noisy. 

i've not always been this fickle with other cafes in the past. but, this time, i had no reason to be patient with the cafe's shortcomings. upon reflection, i realised that this cafe had merely been transactional with me in all my experiences with it. and, in the absence of them investing in a relationship with me, i felt as (un)attached to them as any other semi-decent cafe in the vicinity. so, whereas i may have been patient with some other cafes when they slipped-up (and perhaps even romanticised some of those imperfections), this cafe's magic-trick fell apart at the smallest tear in the experience. 

ultimately, i know my unease was not really about venture-capital or consultants : it was simply about me being sensitive to an erosion in care, relatability, and _quality_[^3] in my lived experience. in just the recent past, i've felt this unease with universities inflating class-sizes, students obsessing over grades, corporations prioritising profit over everything, human-resources teams not really caring about employees, consultants slapping cookie-cutter processes into everything they can, youtube videos feeling less authentic, films and songs feeling catchy but shallow, and so much more. as more experiences in the world grow more transactional and less relational, the once-subtle feeling of unease has grown into a loud continuous knell in my head. this cafe just happened to be the straw that broke my back and forced me to confront that unease.

there's obviously nothing inherently wrong with wanting to succeed. the trouble lies in the nature of the word "success", in a systemic failure to let people feel "content", and in a fanatical obsession with equating success with the idea of "growth". furthermore, those issues are exacerbated by the fickle relationship between words and meanings. for example, the word "cafe" can mean different things to different people, and even different things to the same person under different circumstances. so, a lot of care must go into *aligning* words with meanings with expectations with reality. 

when we notice founders describe their ventures with words that don't match our experience of their companies, we're experiencing that misalignment in action. as customers or patrons or recipients of "an experience"[^6], we experience this misalignment as a _loss_ in many forms: in care, in quality, in attention, in detail, in service, in reliability, in trust, in a relationship, et cetera.

in my life, i experience this misalignment as a dissonance ; as a tear in the matrix ; as a foul smell hanging in the air. the dissonance is like background static in the harmony of my life, with each experience of misalignment adding to the cacophony or making it louder. this is what makes me uneasy, irritable, and finally upset. some people learn to drown it out by _playing_ their lives louder, and others grow tone-deaf to that static's frequencies with age. i can't seem to.
