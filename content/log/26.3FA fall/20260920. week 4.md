#### [[uxfun (26.3FA)]]
based on students' unexpected(ly good) progress last week, i skipped introducing concepts like *affordances* (from don norman's book, design of everyday things)—for now!—, and threw them into a project instead. students formed teams, and here-on will work as designers to identify and improve *something* in a grocery-store's experience. i asked them to: 
1. monday:
	1. list out all their preconceptions (about features and problems); vomit out our prejudices, preconceptions, and assumptions; so that we can dive into the project with an **open mind**.
	2. perform **secondary study** from sources of information (eg. research papers, consumer psychology texts, reddit threads, etc) and learn about why grocery stores are designed the way they are, and what their well-known features or problems are. 
	3. choose a store, and visit it to perform **unstructured observations**; get a feel of the place, so to speak. 
2. wednesday:
	1. collect all their observations, and then organise their unstructured data through **affinity maps** or even predefined categories (by using **aeiou** — activities, environments, interactions, objects, and ~~users~~ people).
	2. since students hadn't paid much attention to people during their unstructured observations, we spent some time discussing *people*: we noted down all the things we might want to know more about them, and categorised *how* we'd go about satisfying our curiosity.
	   ![[Pasted image 20260918200815.png]]
	   finally, we discussed some **observation** etiquette (i.e., for watching people), so that we can perform **semi-structured observations** as homework over the weekend.
	3. my hope is that they return next week with enough information to be able to select a persona/archetype (i.e., **who** they want to design for), so that they can eventually go off and practice **interviewing** people. i wonder what they will go for: the obvious choice (i.e., young college-going students), or select something more adventurous (eg. people who are either elderly, are disabled or injured, don't own cars, have dietary restrictions, come to stores in groups, have families or young children, are health conscious, etc)?

feedback from [[sienna wheeler]]: i could try harder to involve non-participating students in class activities. i feel like this will be less of an issue from this week on, because students will be working in groups (and it's easier to work on balance within 5-member groups than it is in 20-member classrooms), but it was still good feedback. she also brought some nice ideas for how i can distribute students into groups (eg. by asking them to make pairs first, and then clubbing pairs randomly) instead of assigning them into groups completely at-random.
