### teaching work

#### [[exlab (26.3FA)]]

we're itching to start sketching ideas. however, when we impulsively start exploring ideas (through hand-drawn sketches, [[gen't]] prompts, etc), we quickly reach a point where we can not objectively assess or prioritise *which* of those ideas to resolve further. 

this is usually because our approach ==lacks focus==. 

my job as a teacher is to provide the structure that helps students achieve that focus. so—

this week, i forced students to stick to ==words==: to write focused problem-statements (using the format described for [jira stories](https://www.atlassian.com/agile/project-management/user-stories) or [hmw statements](https://ixdf.org/literature/topics/how-might-we) as starting points); define specific and narrow goals; and substantiate everything with evidence (eg. images, quotes, journey-maps, storyboards to illustrate the problem) and insights.

basically: students were pushed to ==burst open== statements like "make the interface more efficient" by first defining what terms like "efficient" meant in their context (say, 'to complete said task in half the time, with fewer clicks, and a lower chance for making errors'), and then identifying which ==heuristics== were violated (and when and how often). 

their broad initial goal, then, became ==many small goals==: so that, from next week, students would prioritise and fix those many small heuristic-violations *one at a time*, and at each step along the way we'd all be able to use an ==objective vocabulary== to discuss each exploration (eg. by asking "does this reduce clicks" instead of "how is this efficient").

#### [[uxfun (26.3FA)]]

students made storyboards to communicate two things: the problems they'd identified, and solutions that they [[261004. week 6#uxfun (26.3FA)|sketched last week]].

![[261005-214058-_A050418.jpg]]

and then each group presented its work to the class to bring the project to an end. 

i've attended presentations in other classes before and noticed how students in the audience are disengaged when others present. i don't like this because students lose opportunities to reflect or share learnings; plus, it creates the wrong impression that instructors make the best advisors. overall, presentations tend to be drab affairs. presentations should be an opportunity for students to learn from each other instead of being trivialised as a "we did work, so give us grades" exercise. so, over the past year—first with [[mustafa arshad]]'s help, and now with [[yanhong lu]] and [[sienna wheeler]]'s inputs—i've structured 'presentation' classes in such a way that all students contribute to each other's learning on the day. (we used reflective questions, post-its, group-discussions, and a bell. and no one looked at a screen while their peers presented. *whee.*)

#### [[behaviour hacking project (26–27)]]

in this week's session, we deconstructed a ritual (into its actors, goals, objects, actions, etc), and then 'hacked' it by changing one or two things in it, à la [[decomputation]].

here's one example: if we have a ritual whose goal was to help a person calm down *before a class*, can we use the same ritual to help them de-focus (and become playful or excited for an evening party, perhaps)? what are the fewest changes we can make to it to adapt the ritual for this new goal? (for example: could we change the sequence of events, by cutting-up the storyboard's frames and moving them around? could we instead change one object, or maybe just action the person performs?)
