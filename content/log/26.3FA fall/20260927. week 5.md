#### [[uxfun (26.3FA)]]
we began the week with a discussion on how a 'shooting star' can be an astronaut's poop — and how it is important to look at every situation from multiple perspectives (eg. a romantic couple looking at a shooting star; an astronaut who does and also doesn't give a shit about it; or a dinosaur who has ptsd from a certain past event involving a shooting star that didn't fully burn-up in the atmosphere). 

we then practised making **personas/archetypes**. i warned students against treating *techniques* as *constraints*, and to keep updating persons as they *sensemake* through the project. we also touched-upon "[weird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology#WEIRD_bias)"-ness, but only really touched-upon it (and nothing more).

and then came the part where they learn about **interviews**. i turned the class into a big market, with half the students pretending to be shoppers and the other half practising interviewing them. i was nervous that students wouldn't respond to it, but they did. (whee.) i saw them open up (and be less shy when talking to people), not be afraid to go with their gut (and make all the mistakes one can), and make adjustments as i went-around giving feedback. heck, i even heard laughter. 

feedback from [[sienna wheeler]]: i should start using brightspace ~~more~~ (to share assignments, course deadlines, and task reminders with students), because students rely on the software to track their workload across multiple courses.

#### other

i'd made a poster for the "ux callout" last week ([[20260920. week 4#other|see]]). this week, we had the callout. it was meant to be for students to learn about their faculty, and for students to mingle among each other (especially so that the incoming freshmen get to interact with older students). i'd carried my camera—hoping to click fun photos for the programme's archives—, but i didn't have anything to photograph because almost no students turned up. so, i mulled about the culture in this programme; or the lack thereof. 

in the callout, one student asked how we grade student-work. the things is, no one asks such a question unless they come from a place of fear ; so, i was hoping we (as faculty) would find a creative way to gift the student courage instead of directly answering them, or at-least deflect it elegantly (by saying something silly like "we're a creative programme with diverse and creative faculty-members, and so our grading systems are diverse, not unfair, and always in the students' interests"). unfortunately, we didn't. 

students keep coming to me asking to give feedback on their portfolios. before i even look at their portfolios, i simply ask them what they enjoy doing, what they feel like they're good at, or any work that they are proud of. they either go blank, or break down, or basically give a boilerplate response ("i like discovering empathy in order to improve human lives" — *vomit*). 

there's so much fear in the air, you can taste it if you stick your tongue out. it manifests as disengagement, passiveness, laziness, templatisation, and in so many other ways ; but, fundamentally, it is fear. why are we unable, as instructors, to gift students courage? we give students rubrics, deadlines, feedback, grades, and other things and proudly claim to be succeeding in *our jobs*, but are we fulfilling our moral *duty* toward students?

later in the week, i got a message from a student ([this](_attachments/Screenshot%202026-09-25%20at%203.21.22%20pm.png)), which made me feel like maybe i'm not entirely crazy, and that i shouldn't let others control how i express myself (in my role as a teacher).
